Plexus: Design

A symmetry-routed exact classifier: it files every shape by what it is, not where it sits.

0. The task

The input is a binary grid: an int[] of rows × cols cells, each 0 or 1. Each grid carries a label, 1 if it belongs to a target class and 0 otherwise. The classes are defined by shape: a shape and its turns, mirror images and moves are usually one class, but the data decides that, shape by shape. Sometimes a shape's orientation or position is the class (a side T against an upright T, a dot in the corner against a dot in the centre), and the router has to keep those apart when, and only when, the labels say so.

The hard case is contradiction: two grids that agree on every coordinate the router reads but carry opposite labels. Plexus handles them exactly. Where the trailing coordinates (orientation, pose, corner) separate them, they do; where different shapes collide, a new shape coordinate is inserted if one of the battery's primitives tells them apart; and what is left (the same grid trained with both labels, or different shapes no primitive separates) stays as stored grids at the leaf, each answering with its own majority, never smoothed over.

1. What it is

Plexus is a router, not a network. It has no weights, no thresholds, no statistics. Every grid is described (§2), filed under a key in one of two tries (§3), and read back with one of four verdicts.

    grid ──describe──▶ D4 root / D6 root → box → symmetry walk → mass → joins → [extensions] → orientation → pose → corner

 Part  What it does 
 Descriptor  bounding box, lattice pick (square or hex), canonical pose, the symmetry walk's answers, mass, joins, and the trailing coordinates 
 The trie  two tries, training, the four verdicts and the extrapolation setting 
 Self-extension  a new shape coordinate where different shapes collide; [8] diagnose 
 Slices  constraints per level, a callback per grid, constructive generation 
 The interpreter  cli.Main: score, trie, grid, slice, diagnose 
 The engine  lazy grids, templates in every pose, stages folded over a grid, for a domain layer to drive 

The unifying idea is the slice: fix some coordinates of the key and read what stays constant, or enumerate what varies. Classification runs the key forward (grid to answer); a slice runs it backward (constraints to every grid that answers so). The same tries serve both.

The Descriptor

Everything Plexus reads off one grid, computed once.

2. The descriptor

Descriptor.of(grid, rows, cols) turns a grid into the values the key is built from. It works on the shape's own crop, so where the shape sits in the frame enters only at the last coordinate.

2.1 The bounding box

One pass over the pixels gives the box of the non-zero cells: a square (h = w) or a rectangle. An empty grid has no box and gets the single key empty.

The box caps the symmetry the shape can have. Any symmetry of a shape also maps its box onto itself, so it fixes the box's centre, and the centre is the only point the shape can be symmetric about. A rectangle allows at most D2 (rot180 and the two axis mirrors); a quarter turn or a diagonal mirror would turn an h × w box into w × h.

2.2 Picking the lattice

A shape lives on one of two lattices, and each has its own trie.

1. Rectangle → square lattice, always. The hex lattice is never tried on a rectangle. A 60° or 120° turn cycles the three hex axes, so a shape with one needs equal extents on all three, and its box on the square input is square.
2. Square box → measure both diagonals. The width across the anti-diagonal is the spread of row + column; across the main diagonal, the spread of row − column. If a width equals the side, shearing the rows makes that diagonal a hex neighbour direction:
   - row + column matches → shift the rows right: square cell (r, c) becomes axial (q = c, r), and anti-diagonal neighbours become adjacent;
   - row − column matches → shift them left (mirror the columns, then shift right), and main-diagonal neighbours become adjacent;
   - both match → take both shifts and keep the one whose form up to D6 is smaller. Every pose of the shape then makes the same choice.
3. Compare. Hex wins only with strictly more symmetry than the square lattice gives the same shape; a tie stays square.

 Shape  Square symmetries  Hex symmetries  Lattice 
 plus .x./xxx/.x.  8  4  square 
 hex disk .xx/xxx/xx.  4  12  hex 
 solid 3×3  8  (anti-diagonal width 5, not 3: not tried)  square 

The shift is one cell to one cell, nothing interpolated, so the input stays exact. The disk's 90° turn, xx./xxx/.xx, matches the other diagonal, shifts the other way, and lands on the same hexagon.

Lattices. A router is built on a set of lattices, fixed for its life, because the neighbours are part of the key: they define joins and every self-extension primitive. Lattice.SQUARE_4 (edge neighbours), Lattice.SQUARE_8 (edge or corner neighbours, the king's move) and Lattice.HEX_6 (the six neighbours of the sheared reading). A legal set holds exactly one square lattice, since rectangles always file on the square lattice and square joins must have one meaning, and optionally HEX_6; anything else throws IllegalArgumentException. The default is SQUARE_4 + HEX_6.

    new Plexus(rows, cols)                                    // SQUARE_4 + HEX_6, self-extension on
    new Plexus(rows, cols, Lattice.SQUARE_8)                  // square only, 8 neighbours
    new Plexus(rows, cols, Lattice.SQUARE_8, Lattice.HEX_6)   // 8-neighbour squares, hex still tried
    new Plexus(rows, cols, Set.of(Lattice.SQUARE_4), false)   // square only, self-extension off

Without HEX_6, steps 2 and 3 are skipped for every square box: each shape stays on the square lattice, files under the D4 root, and the D6 root stays empty. The hex disk then reads as a square box with rot180, diagMain and diagAnti. Descriptor.of(grid, rows, cols, lattices) describes one grid the same way; every descriptor records the lattice that described it in lattice().

2.3 The symmetry walk

The walk asks one question per symmetry, yes or no, in a fixed order: turns first, smallest angle first, then mirrors in the order the table gives (by axis angle on hex). A turn whose inverse is already asked (rot270 on D4, rot240 and rot300 on D6) always gets the same answer and is left out.

 Kind  Group  Questions 
 rectangle  D2  rot180, short-axis mirror, long-axis mirror 
 square box  D4  rot90, rot180, mirrorH, mirrorV, diagMain, diagAnti 
 hex  D6  rot60, rot120, rot180, mirror0, mirror30, mirror60, mirror90, mirror120, mirror150 

Symmetries combine (two perpendicular mirrors force rot180; rot90 forces rot180), so some answers are implied by earlier ones. Those levels simply have one child.

2.4 The canonical pose

The questions must not depend on how the shape arrived, so the walk is asked of a canonical pose:

- Rectangle: turned to landscape first, the envelope, then the smallest of its four D2 images. In landscape the short-axis mirror is the one that swaps the two ends of the long side; a T always has exactly that mirror, whichever way it arrived.
- Square box and hex: the pose whose walk answers say yes as early as possible in the order above, ties to the smallest image. A tall T is walked with its mirror as mirrorH, never mirrorV.

2.5 Invariants and trailing coordinates

On the canonical pose:

- mass: filled cells;
- joins: adjacent pairs on the shape's own lattice: 4 or 8 neighbours square (SQUARE_4 or SQUARE_8), 6 hex.

Then the three trailing coordinates, which record how and where the shape arrived:

 Coordinate  Rectangle  Square box  Hex 
 orientation  envelope turn: 0 not turned, 1 turned  none (−1)  shift direction: 0 right, 1 left 
 pose  which of 4 D2 transforms reached the canonical pose (0–3)  which of 8 D4 transforms (0–7)  which of 12 D6 transforms (0–11) 
 corner  raw top-left corner of the box in the input frame  same  same 

The pose is the lowest-numbered transform that reaches the canonical pose. A symmetric shape reaches it by several, so only  group  ÷  stabilizer  codes occur: 4 for an L on a rectangle, 2 for a T, 1 for a bar, the plus or the hex disk.

2.6 Companion planes

A grid often comes with other planes of the same frame: the other faces of a board, a mask of what is known. descriptor.toCanonical(plane, ...) moves them exactly as the shape was moved (cropped at its corner, enveloped or sheared, then turned or mirrored into the canonical pose) and returns a Fold.

    final var d = plexus.describe(ones);
    final Fold fold = d.toCanonical(ones, twos, unknown);   // one motion for all three planes
    fold.cells(1);                                           // the twos, as {a, b} in the canonical frame

- One motion for every plane. Where the planes sit relative to the shape and to each other is kept. Coordinates are those of the canonical pose, with the shape starting at (0, 0); companion cells can lie outside its box and be negative.
- Symmetric shapes. Several motions reach the canonical pose of a symmetric shape and each puts the companions somewhere different. All are tried and the smallest fold is kept. On hex, a shape that reads as the same hexagon sheared either way (a dot, the disk) is tried with both shears, because a quarter turn of the frame swaps the two diagonals.
- Result. Turning, mirroring or moving the whole frame never changes the fold. Two inputs fold equal exactly when one motion of the lattice carries the shape and every companion onto the other's.
- Empty shape. No canonical pose exists; the planes come back unmoved, in input coordinates.

The Trie

Two tries, one per lattice; training, reading, and the extrapolation setting.

3. The trie

3.1 Layout

    D4 root                                   D6 root
    └─ box (short × long, or empty)           └─ box (the common extent)
       └─ rot180? / rot90? ...                   └─ rot60? ...
          └─ ... one level per symmetry             └─ ...
             └─ mass
                └─ joins
                   └─ [extensions]
                      └─ orientation
                         └─ pose
                            └─ corner   (leaf: the trained grids)

A shape is filed under its lattice's root for good. Every node has one parent, prefixes are shared, and every node keeps two counts: how many training grids labelled 0 and 1 passed through it. So every partial key (a box, a box plus some symmetry answers, a mass within those) is a node with its own evidence.

Everything above the trailing levels describes the shape alone: every turn, mirror and move of a shape shares that part of the path. The trailing levels come last so that a read only needs them where labels disagree across poses or positions.

3.2 Training

Training walks the grid's whole path, creating any missing nodes, adds one to the counts of every node passed, and stores the grid at the leaf. Nothing is sealed. fit inserts every grid in one pass and then runs self-extension (§4) once; accept inserts one grid, runs self-extension, and is legal at any time.

3.3 Reading

A read follows the grid's path until it reaches a leaf or breaks off at a missing child.

 Verdict  When 
 POSITIVE / NEGATIVE  the whole path was trained. The leaf answers: a grid stored there answers with its own majority, any other grid with the leaf's 
 ASSUMED  the path breaks off at a pure node (one label only, and not a root) that the extrapolation setting lets stand in for the missing child. The answer is that node's label 
 UNSEEN  the path breaks off where no stand-in is allowed: a mixed node, or a pure node above what the setting allows. Not a negative: an absence of evidence 

3.4 Extrapolation

Extrapolate sets how far a read may go beyond what was trained: the highest level a pure node may stand in at. Each setting allows everything the one before it does. The same setting governs slices (§5).

 Extrapolate  A pure node stands in for 
 NONE  nothing 
 PLACEMENT  unseen orientations, poses and corners of trained shapes 
 SYMMETRY (default)  also unseen symmetry classes, provided the query's mass and joins occur together somewhere under the node 
 EVERYTHING  also unseen mass, joins and extension values, and unseen symmetry classes whatever their mass and joins 

The root never stands in for a box, so a grid whose box was never trained is UNSEEN under every setting. Under the default, a fold over symmetry never crosses mass or joins: trained on the T (mass 4, joins 3), the L (xxx/x.., also 4 and 3) is ASSUMED 1, while the U (x.x/xxx, mass 5) and x.x/x.x (mass 4, joins 0) are UNSEEN. Under EVERYTHING both are ASSUMED 1.

A pure node's children record which values were actually seen, and the answer's context shows how deep the evidence goes. With only upright T's trained, a side T is ASSUMED from the joins=3 node, whose children split on orientation: no side T was ever seen. With a side T also trained, a side T at a new corner is ASSUMED from turned=yes → pose=0: only the corner is new. Train a side T with label 0 instead and the orientation level separates them: side T's then read 0 and upright T's 1, for this shape only.

Self-Extension

A new shape coordinate where different shapes collide.

4. Self-extension

A mixed node means one of two things:

- One shape, different labels by pose or place. Upright T's labelled 1 and side T's labelled 0; a dot in the corner labelled 1 and the same dot in the centre labelled 0. The trailing levels (orientation, pose, corner) already separate these. Nothing is added.
- Different shapes that share the key so far. A path and a fork with the same box, symmetry, mass and joins. What is missing is a shape coordinate.

Self-extension runs at the end of fit and after every accept; extend() runs it on demand. Every junction (the node just above the trailing levels) that holds both labels is checked by comparing its members' canonical poses. If they are several shapes, the battery primitive with the best information gain, and not already on the path, is inserted at the junction, and the subtree is rebuilt from the stored grids. A primitive is only inserted if its gain is positive. This repeats until no junction changes. new Plexus(rows, cols, lattices, false) turns the automatic runs off.

4.1 The battery

Primitive.BATTERY holds properties of the shape's cell graph on the lattice that described it, the same adjacency as its joins (4 or 8 neighbours square, 6 hex):

 Primitive  Value 
 max-degree  the most neighbours any cell has 
 branch-points  cells with 3 or more neighbours 
 end-points  cells with exactly 1 neighbour 
 pieces  connected components 
 loops  independent cycles: joins − mass + pieces 

None of them refers to a direction, so each is unchanged by every turn and mirror of its lattice and is a legal coordinate on both tries. Ties go to the earlier primitive in the list. On separable-path-fork-5x5 Plexus inserts max-degree: the fork has a cell with 3 neighbours, the path does not.

4.2 Diagnose

Plexus.diagnose() ([8] in the interpreter) lists the mixed junctions and the mixed leaves, each with its path and counts, and what resolves it. Mixed nodes elsewhere (above a junction, or between it and the leaves) are not listed. With nothing to list it prints none: every node is pure.

 Report  Where  Meaning 
 one shape: separated by orientation, pose or corner  junction  the trailing levels split the labels 
 several shapes: separated by <primitive>  junction  an extension was inserted here 
 several shapes: no primitive separates them; kept as members at the leaves  junction  the battery has nothing with positive gain 
 the same grid trained with both labels  leaf  a true contradiction; a read answers with the grid's majority label, and a tie falls to the leaf's majority 
 different shapes with one full key: each stored grid answers with its own label  leaf  shapes that agree on every level down to the corner, and that no primitive separated 

Slices

Constraints per level, a callback per grid, and constructive generation.

5. Slices

A slice walks one root or both under a constraint per level and streams every matching grid to a callback.

    plexus.slice(
        Slice.on(Root.D4)                      // Root.D4, Root.D6, or Root.BOTH
             .box(2, 3)                        // omit for any box
             .atLeast("short-axis mirror")     // or .exactly(...)
             .mass(4, 5)                       // a range; omit for any
             .joins(3, 4)
             .label(1)                         // omit for both labels
             .orientation(0).pose(0)           // placement constraints; omit for any
             .corner(0, 0)
             .per(Slice.Per.PLACEMENT)         // the default; or POSE, or SHAPE
             .extrapolate(Extrapolate.SYMMETRY),  // the default
        (grid, context, evidence) -> {
            // grid:     the shape, posed and placed in the input frame
            // context:  the node that controls its label: path, counts, answer, root, depth, boundary
            // evidence: CONFIRMED or ASSUMED
        });

5.1 Constraints

- Box: short × long on D4 (order does not matter), or the extent on D6.
- Symmetries follow the walk. A required symmetry takes only the yes edge; an unmentioned one takes both; exactly takes the no edge for every symmetry not listed (list a closed set: two perpendicular mirrors bring rot180). Symmetry names belong to a kind of box: short-axis mirror to rectangles, mirrorH to square boxes, mirror30 to hex. A required name the box's walk does not have rules that box out, so most names pick a kind of box and with it a root. rot180 is in all three walks: atLeast("rot180") over Root.BOTH returns rectangles, square boxes and hex shapes alike. Names are matched without regard to case, spaces or hyphens (long-axis-mirror means long-axis mirror); a name no walk asks about throws IllegalArgumentException listing the names, which Slice.symmetryNames() also returns.
- Mass, joins: ranges.
- Label: 1, 0, or both.
- Orientation, pose, corner: the placement levels. Orientation is 0 or 1 (on a rectangle: not turned or turned; on hex: rows shifted right or left); a square box has none, so an orientation constraint rules square boxes out. Pose is the pose code, with 0 the canonical pose itself. Corner is the row and column of the box's top-left in the input frame.
- Per: what one delivered entry stands for (§5.2).
- Extrapolate: how far the slice may go beyond what was trained (§3.4).

5.2 What comes back

- CONFIRMED: every trained grid on a matching path, once each, delivered as found, with its leaf as the context. The label constraint is checked against the answer a read of that grid gives.
- ASSUMED: every grid a pure node within the setting stands in for, and whose label the constraint keeps, delivered after the walk: the deepest node first, and at equal depth the nodes whose parent is mixed first, since those sit next to disagreement and are the best to check.

Slice.Result counts the confirmed and assumed entries and the nodes skipped as too large to generate.

By default every matching grid is an entry (Per.PLACEMENT). Per.POSE folds the positions together and delivers one grid per distinct pose of each shape; Per.SHAPE also folds the poses and delivers one grid per distinct shape. The grid delivered for a group is a trained one if the group has any (so the entry is CONFIRMED), and among those the one nearest the canonical pose and the frame's top-left: not turned, lowest pose code, lowest corner. On the eight T's of conservation-t-orbit-3x3, with label 1 and Extrapolate.NONE: 8 placements, 4 poses, 1 shape, the last shown as xxx/.x. in the top-left corner. Folded entries arrive together after the walk.

5.3 Constructive generation

For a stand-in at a shape level (symmetry, mass, joins, extension), candidates are built, not searched for:

1. Read the box and the symmetries already answered yes on the path.
2. Group the box's cells into the orbits of those symmetries.
3. Fill whole orbits only: any shape with those symmetries has each orbit all filled or all empty.
4. Expand each candidate over every pose and placement in the input frame, and keep the grids whose read would break off at exactly this node, and which the setting and the query admit.

A one-mirror 2×3 node has 4 orbits, so 2⁴ fills instead of 2⁶; a fully symmetric 3×3 node has 3, so 2³ instead of 2⁹. For stand-ins at the trailing levels, the candidates are every pose and placement of the shapes stored under the node. On the hex root the candidates are all subsets of the s×s square input box, without the orbit step. A node whose enumeration would exceed 2²¹ is skipped and counted in Slice.Result: more than 21 orbits on the square root, or a box larger than 4×4 on the hex root.

What a slice generates is exactly what reading answers for. The suite checks this on three 3×3 datasets, for every setting and both labels, against all 512 grids of the frame.

6. The interpreter

    java -cp out org.tervel.plexus.cli.Main data/conservation-t-orbit-3x3.txt --lattice square8

    [5] score  [t] trie  [4] grid  [s] slice  [8] diagnose  [q] quit

 Command  What it does 
 [5] score  reads every training grid back under the default setting and counts those answered with their own label

The Engine

Lazy grids, templates matched in every pose, and stages folded over a grid: what a domain layer drives.

7. The engine

The router classifies one grid. The engine runs a program over a grid: a caller (a domain layer such as a screen grammar) describes its problem as planes, templates and stages, and the engine evaluates it. Nothing here knows what a label means; the caller's vocabulary says.

7.1 Grid

A Grid is rows × cols cells, each carrying one label, supplied by an Oracle the caller gives:

    Grid g = Grid.of(rows, cols, vocabulary, (r, c) -> recognize(r, c));

- Lazy. A cell's label is computed the first time something needs it and remembered. A cell nothing asks about is never computed. recognized() says how many were.
- Immutable. relabel(r, c, label) and mark(plane, r, c, note) return a new grid. Every grid derived from one capture shares what the oracle answered, so no cell is computed twice.
- Planes. in(plane, r, c) resolves a plane name through the Vocabulary, in order: a plane that holds everywhere; a plane a stage marked the cell in; the cell's label; a class of labels. Off the grid only the vocabulary's outside planes hold. The first two answer without computing the label.
- Read and written stay apart. A label is either read (the oracle observed it) or a state (a stage concluded it); the vocabulary's states names the second kind. read(r, c) is always the oracle's answer and label(r, c) the current one. relabel writes only states, and an oracle answering with a state is an error, so a conclusion can never pass for an observation.

 Vocabulary part  Meaning 
 classes  class name → the labels it groups 
 values  label → the integer it carries (value(r, c)) 
 outside  plane names that hold for a cell off the grid 
 everywhere  plane names that hold for every cell on the grid 
 states  labels only a stage writes and the oracle never answers 

7.2 Template

A Template is a small pattern of named cells around an anchor:

    Template t = Template.of(new String[][]{
            {"clear",   "clear",   "clear"},
            {"one",     "two",     "one"},
            {"covered", "covered", "covered"}}, 2, 1);   // anchor: row 2, column 1

- Cropped by construction. It is stored as offsets from the anchor; null cells are not part of it, so its size is whatever it was declared with. box() gives its extent.
- Poses. Its images under the square's eight motions about the anchor, the anchor staying put. Images that coincide are kept once, so a symmetric template has  D4  ÷  Stab  poses. Each pose records the move that produced it from the declaration. The smallest pose is canonical(); two templates are equal when their canonical poses are, so a template and its rotation are the same template.
- Containment. matches(grid, r, c) is true when, in some pose with the anchor on (r, c), every cell is in the plane it names. Cells it does not name are never looked at, so the grid may carry more. The first cell that fails rejects the pose.
- Placements. covering(grid, r, c, role) lists every placement that holds and has a cell named role on (r, c): the ways the template can sit with that cell inside it. Placement.count(grid, role, plane) counts how many of its role cells are in plane, and at(plane) gives where its cells named plane landed.

7.3 Expression

Expression.parse("value - flag"): integers and names joined by + and -. eval(name -> …) takes what each name is worth from the caller.

7.4 Stages and pipelines

A Stage<C> is a context and a grid in, a grid out. The output may be the input unchanged; the caller assumes nothing and hands it on. The context C is the caller's: whatever it resolved before this grid existed. Plexus never looks inside it, and hands the same one to every stage, test and effect of one application.

    Stage<Run> forced = Stage.atEach("covered", (run, in, r, c) -> …condition…, (run, out, r, c) -> out.relabel(r, c, "flag"));
    Pipeline<Run> solve = Stage.of(List.of(forced, freed, guess.onlyIf((run, g) -> !g.anyMarked("click"))));
    Grid result = solve.apply(run, grid);

- Stage.atEach(anchor, test, effect) visits every cell of the anchor plane. Each test reads the grid the stage was given, so the order cells are visited in cannot change the result.
- Stage.sweep(anchor, test, effect) visits the same cells but writes through: each test reads the grid as the stage has written it so far, so a change is seen by every cell visited after it, and a cell an earlier change took out of anchor is skipped.
- onlyIf(predicate) runs a stage only when the predicate holds for its context and input.
- A Pipeline folds the grid through its stages in order, once, in one context. A pipeline is itself a stage.

7.5 Frames and shape counts

- Frame.stabilizer(grid, rows, cols) gives the motions of a fixed frame that leave a grid of any integers unchanged, as a mask over Frame.moves(rows, cols) (eight on a square frame, four on a rectangle). Unlike a descriptor it compares cell values in place.
- Descriptor.stabilizerSize() and orbitSize() count a shape's own symmetry wherever it sits: a domino has 4 motions and 2 orientations.

7.6 Repeats

Repeat.of(grid, rows, cols) gives the whole shifts that map a grid of cells onto itself, as Descriptor gives the turns and mirrors. The grid is binary or labelled: 0 is background, any other value a label. A picture of pixels is read into cells first (more than 64 labels is refused); repeats at a fractional number of pixels are the reader's business, not Plexus's. Everything is exact.

- Agreement at a shift is the share of labelled cells whose cell one shift over holds the same label, counted exactly (through Plexus's own FFT, rounded). Prominence is how far that stands from chance (how often the labels would agree if they were scattered independently) towards perfect: a repeat is a peak at a prominence of at least a quarter, relative because a pattern filling a third of the frame agrees far less overall than one filling all of it, and random cells agree as often as they are dense. A whole multiple of a shorter repeat is taken as the shorter one when every multiple of that shorter step up to it is at least half as prominent: a short shift that agrees only because cells clump together fails somewhere along that chain.
- Every cell. A one-cell step sits beside the zero shift, which agrees perfectly, so it is never a peak. Where no peak is found, the grid repeats every cell when one label clearly fills it (more than half the frame and more than twice any other label): the motif is that one label and everything else is a defect. A board read into cells is this case: the covered cell is the motif, the opened cells the defects, grouped by face. A lone shape in an empty frame, or random cells, does not repeat.
- The basis v1, v2 is reduced to the shortest pair, v1 pointing right and v2 down; when they are equally long v1 is the more horizontal; on a hex lattice v2 is the neighbour at 60°. So the same pattern always gets the same (i, j) addresses.
- The type is read from exact lengths and angles on the grid's storage: the square grid's own, or for Repeat.of(grid, rows, cols, Lattice.HEX_6) the hex plane's, where a hex lattice is exact. SQUARE, HEX, RECTANGULAR, RHOMBIC, OBLIQUE, or STRIPE (one direction) and NONE. moves() lists the moves that map it onto itself, as Plexus's own D4 and D6 in their own order: all 8 for square, 12 for hex, identity, rot180, mirrorH, mirrorV for rectangular, identity, rot180, diagMain, diagAnti for rhombic, identity, rot180 for oblique. lattice() is HEX_6 for hex, SQUARE_4 for the rest.
- Repeat.basis(v1, v2, storage, …) builds one from known steps; as(type) reads it as a declared type, which wins over what was measured.

repeat.periods(grid, rows, cols, sizes, picker) splits the grid into a frame and its defects:

- A period is the  v1 × v2  cells the repeat's parallelogram covers from a corner.
- Where a period starts is not in the repeat: every one of its corners tiles the grid. Each corner is ranked by how many of the repeat's moves leave the motif unchanged about the centre of the period it starts, and the most symmetric are the candidates (phases()). A tile's corner and the crossing of the gaps between tiles can be equally symmetric, so the caller's Picker chooses; NEAREST_TOP_LEFT when nothing tells them apart.
- The region is a block of whole periods, each scored by the share of its cells holding the motif's label. With sizes the block of each allowed size that stands highest above the ring of periods round it wins, so the size is exact; without, the largest connected block of the high group, the groups split where the data splits (only when the split explains at least 78% of the spread, above the 64% of noise round one value and the 75% of evenly spread values).
- The motif is the most frequent label at each period cell over the region (a first one, to rank the corners, over the periods that agree with their neighbours). A defect is a period that differs from it anywhere, and defects holding exactly the same labels share a group. score and distance (differing cells) are returned for every period.
